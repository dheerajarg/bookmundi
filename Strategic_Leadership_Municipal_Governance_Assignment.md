Strategic Leadership, Municipal Governance and Sustainability in Nepal:
A Mixed-Method Review of Kathmandu Metropolitan City, Dharan, Lalitpur, Dhangadhi and Pokhara

1.0 Introduction

The return of elected local governments after federal restructuring has changed the political and administrative landscape of Nepal. Since the Constitution of Nepal 2015 institutionalized federalism, local governments have no longer remained only service outlets of the central state; they have increasingly become political, managerial, and developmental arenas where leadership matters directly. Municipalities such as Kathmandu Metropolitan City, Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City, Lalitpur Metropolitan City, Dhangadhi Sub-Metropolitan City, and Pokhara Metropolitan City have become visible examples of how mayoral leadership can influence governance reform, civic trust, administrative responsiveness, and sustainable urban transformation. In recent years, the public debate in Nepal has increasingly focused not only on what municipalities do, but on how current mayors lead, mobilize resources, use public legitimacy, and shape the governance culture of their local governments.

This assignment critically analyzes the role of strategic leadership in changing the governance of municipalities in Nepal, with special focus on Kathmandu Metropolitan City, Dharan, Lalitpur, Dhangadhi, and Pokhara. The central question is whether current municipal leadership has merely improved visibility and political popularity, or whether it has also strengthened corporate governance principles and corporate sustainability in a meaningful way. Since municipalities are not private corporations, the terms corporate governance and corporate sustainability require contextual interpretation. In this paper, corporate governance is used in an adapted public-sector sense to mean accountability, transparency, strategic coordination, rule-based decision-making, stakeholder responsiveness, ethical stewardship, and performance monitoring. Similarly, corporate sustainability is interpreted as the long-term institutional, financial, environmental, and social sustainability of municipal governance.

This topic is highly relevant in the Nepali context for three reasons. First, after 2017 and especially after the 2022 local elections, several municipalities elected leaders with highly visible personal mandates, including independent mayors. Second, urban local governments in Nepal face growing pressures relating to waste management, environmental stress, mobility, infrastructure, fiscal sustainability, digital governance, public trust, and inclusive service delivery. Third, the leadership discourse in Nepal is often personality-centered, while the policy question is whether leadership is being translated into systems, institutions, and sustainable governance outcomes.

Strategic leadership is different from routine administration. It involves vision-setting, agenda prioritization, coalition-building, adaptive decision-making, resource mobilization, and the ability to align short-term actions with long-term public value. In municipalities, this may be reflected in digital reforms, stakeholder engagement, participatory action, infrastructure prioritization, heritage protection, revenue mobilization, environmental policies, and citizen-facing innovation. Yet strong leadership can also produce contradictions. A highly charismatic mayor may mobilize public participation but weaken institutional procedures. A visionary leader may disrupt old political patterns but create administrative conflict. A popular leader may create immediate results without building a sustainable governance structure. Therefore, the role of strategic leadership needs critical rather than celebratory analysis.

This paper uses a mixed-method design combining a systematic review of relevant literature with comparative secondary analysis of five municipalities. Qualitatively, the study examines themes such as accountability, participation, innovation, public value, and sustainability. Quantitatively, it develops an author-constructed comparative scorecard to assess the relative strength of strategic leadership across the selected municipalities using documentary evidence. This approach is not intended to provide a final ranking of mayors, but to produce a structured and policy-relevant comparison.

The objectives of the study are fourfold. First, to review relevant literature on strategic leadership, municipal governance, and sustainability in Nepal and beyond. Second, to examine how current leaders in selected local governments are changing governance practices. Third, to assess whether these changes have strengthened accountability and sustainability. Fourth, to derive policy implications that may help local governments in Nepal build stronger, more institutionalized, and more sustainable governance systems.

The structure of the paper follows the format required by the assignment. After the introduction, the literature review synthesizes both Nepal-based and international scholarship. The methodology section explains the systematic review and mixed-method design. The analysis section compares the selected municipalities using both narrative evidence and a scorecard. The discussion, conclusion, and implications section then interprets the findings in relation to public policy and municipal governance reform in Nepal.

2.0 Literature Review

2.1 Strategic Leadership in Public and Local Governance

Strategic leadership in public organizations is generally understood as the capacity of senior leaders to create direction, align resources, cope with complexity, and shape long-term outcomes rather than merely supervise routine operations. In the public sector, this idea has moved beyond bureaucratic control and now includes adaptive leadership, public value creation, co-creation, and multi-stakeholder governance. Bryson, Crosby, and Bloomberg (2014) argue that public value governance requires leaders to move beyond traditional public administration and new public management toward collaborative and democratic problem-solving. In other words, public leaders are expected not only to administer rules but also to create collective value in a shared-power environment.

This is especially relevant for municipalities. Local governments work close to citizens, but they also face fragmented authority, political contestation, scarce resources, and rising expectations. Carreri and Payson (2021), writing from the context of U.S. mayors and city managers, show that the quality of local leadership is linked less to formal education alone and more to public service motivation and managerial skill. This is a useful insight for Nepal, where local politics often rewards visibility and charisma, but the sustainability of governance depends on managerial capability, coalition-building, and institutional follow-through.

Strategic leadership is also increasingly linked to sustainability. International work on local government reform shows that municipal leaders must integrate vision, cross-sector coordination, learning, and institutional adaptation if sustainability goals are to move beyond rhetoric. Deslatte and Stokan (2020) argue that local governments face opportunity costs when trying to combine traditional development priorities with sustainability commitments. Municipal leaders therefore need strategic capability to avoid governance silos. Likewise, recent systematic reviews on municipal administrative management toward sustainability emphasize competencies such as holistic leadership, co-creation, institutional learning, and the ability to embed sustainability into routine governance rather than leaving it as a side project.

2.2 Corporate Governance and Its Adaptation to Municipalities

Corporate governance is usually associated with private firms, boards, shareholders, internal controls, and fiduciary responsibility. However, the core principles of corporate governance can be meaningfully applied to public institutions, especially municipalities. These principles include transparency, accountability, oversight, ethical conduct, participation, strategic clarity, risk management, and responsible use of resources. In municipal governance, these principles appear in the form of open meetings, public disclosure, internal control, audit responsiveness, data-driven decision-making, citizen grievance handling, and rule-based administration.

International public governance literature increasingly recognizes that local governments must create public value while also demonstrating disciplined governance comparable to corporate standards. Bryson et al. (2014) emphasize that public value governance still requires institutional responsibility and democratic accountability. Sørensen, Bryson, and Crosby (2021) further argue that public leaders create value most effectively when they mobilize stakeholders through co-creation rather than top-down control. This has direct relevance to Nepali municipalities, where leadership success increasingly depends on whether mayors can engage citizens, the bureaucracy, local business, civil society, and informal urban actors in a coherent governance agenda.

Municipal governance research outside Nepal also suggests that governance success is influenced by strategic coordination, shared vision, and internal control. The public-sector corporate governance literature notes that municipalities may fail not because they lack formal authority, but because they lack strategic coordination between political leadership and administrative systems. This distinction is important in Nepal, where public attention often focuses on mayors, but sustainable governance requires a functioning relationship between elected leadership, chief administrative officers, municipal councils, ward structures, and technical departments.

2.3 Municipal Governance, Accountability and Trust in Nepal

Nepal’s federal transition has created both opportunity and tension for local democracy. Bhusal and Acharya (2024), reviewing five years of local democracy in federal Nepal, argue that local governments have become more visible and politically significant, but their effectiveness remains uneven because of staffing gaps, coordination problems, weak intergovernmental clarity, and uneven institutional capacity. This finding is critical for analyzing current mayors: visible leadership is now more possible than before, but local governments are still constrained by legal ambiguity, bureaucratic shortages, and fiscal dependency.

Han, Aryal, and Hwang (2024) provide one of the most relevant empirical findings for this assignment. Their study shows that transparency and responsiveness significantly predict public trust in local governments in Nepal, while participation strengthens this relationship. This means that leadership matters not simply as symbolic presence, but as a governance mechanism: leaders who improve transparency, responsiveness, and participation are more likely to strengthen public trust. This finding helps explain why some mayors in Nepal have built strong legitimacy even when their formal administrative results remain mixed.

Gyawali’s (2018) comparative study of Kathmandu and Lalitpur metropolitan cities adds a further layer. He found that Kathmandu had stronger ICT infrastructure and human resource capacity, while Lalitpur showed notable opportunities for adaptation and smart governance. The study is useful because it reminds us that governance outcomes do not depend only on individual leaders; municipal institutional conditions also matter. A strategic leader in a weaker system may achieve symbolic breakthroughs, but the institutional depth of the municipality still shapes long-term outcomes.

2.4 Sustainability in Municipal Governance

Municipal sustainability should be understood broadly. It includes environmental sustainability, such as waste management, green mobility, water restoration, and climate responsiveness. It also includes fiscal sustainability, which concerns a municipality’s capacity to mobilize revenue, manage expenditure, and maintain long-term solvency. It further includes institutional sustainability, meaning whether reforms survive beyond a particular mayor’s term.

Thapa et al. (2024) show that the financial condition of municipalities in Nepal remains mostly average, with only a small number performing strongly. This is an important warning. Municipal governance in Nepal is often judged through visible projects or social media presence, but corporate sustainability requires more than visibility; it requires financially viable and administratively durable systems. If municipal leadership is charismatic but fiscally weak, the apparent reform may not be sustainable.

Research on smart city indicators in Lalitpur (Karki & Dahal, 2020) also shows that sustainability requires both infrastructure and institutional readiness. Smart governance is not achieved only through devices, apps, or slogans; it requires policies, skilled human resources, and robust systems. Similarly, research and policy reporting on Pokhara’s waste system show that environmental sustainability requires not just commitment but practical institutional arrangements for segregation, recycling, landfill siting, and partnership with private actors. Thus, sustainability is a strategic governance issue rather than a single-sector program.

2.5 Leadership Trends in Nepal’s Urban Municipalities

Public discourse in Nepal has increasingly focused on “strong mayors,” especially in the post-2022 period. Kathmandu under Balendra Shah, Dharan under Harka Sampang, and Dhangadhi under Gopal Hamal represent cases where independent candidates entered office with reformist images. Lalitpur under Chiri Babu Maharjan, by contrast, illustrates continuity, incremental urban reform, and stronger embeddedness in traditional political networks. Pokhara under Dhana Raj Acharya represents a case where tourism, waste, and infrastructure pressures require leadership that is both politically credible and system-oriented.

The literature and case materials suggest two broad patterns. Independent mayors often excel in agenda disruption, symbolic leadership, and citizen mobilization. Party-based mayors often perform better in intergovernmental negotiation, project continuity, and institutional bargaining. Neither model is automatically superior. The decisive factor is whether leadership becomes institutionalized through procedures, systems, and sustainability-oriented governance.

2.6 Research Gap

Although Nepal has abundant public commentary on mayors and local government, there are limited comparative studies that explicitly connect strategic leadership with corporate governance and sustainability across multiple municipalities. Most discussions remain either journalistic or narrowly focused on one city. There is also limited mixed-method analysis that combines systematic literature review with comparative case assessment. This study addresses that gap by examining five visible municipalities through a common governance lens and by linking Nepali evidence to international public leadership literature.

3.0 Methodology and Material

3.1 Research Design

This study adopts a mixed-method design combining qualitative thematic analysis and a quantitative secondary scorecard. The qualitative component is based on a systematic review of scholarly and policy literature. The quantitative component uses documentary evidence to construct a comparative score across selected municipalities. The mixed-method approach is suitable because the subject of municipal leadership includes both measurable governance signals and interpretive issues such as legitimacy, responsiveness, and sustainability.

3.2 Systematic Literature Review Procedure

The literature review followed a structured search strategy inspired by systematic review principles. Materials were collected from Google Scholar, NepJOL, open-access journal repositories, policy reports, UNDP and World Bank publications, municipal websites, and credible Nepali media sources. Search terms included combinations of “strategic leadership,” “municipal governance,” “local government Nepal,” “public trust,” “sustainability,” “Kathmandu Metropolitan City,” “Dharan,” “Lalitpur,” “Dhangadhi,” and “Pokhara.”

The review focused mainly on the period 2017–2026 because this covers the federal local government era and the current municipal leadership cycle. Older foundational theoretical works were also used where necessary. After removing duplicates and irrelevant items, the final material set consisted of 34 core documents: 18 scholarly articles, 6 policy or institutional reports, and 10 municipal case documents or credible national reports. This balance allowed the paper to combine theoretical grounding with current case evidence.

3.3 Case Selection

Five municipalities were purposively selected because they represent different styles of local leadership and different types of urban governance challenge:

Kathmandu Metropolitan City: national capital, high visibility, independent mayor, intense pressure on governance reform.

Dharan Sub-Metropolitan City: independent mayor, strong participatory image, water crisis and community mobilization.

Lalitpur Metropolitan City: established mayoral leadership, heritage-city management, smart city and environmental initiatives.

Dhangadhi Sub-Metropolitan City: independent mayor, managerial reform orientation, public health and environmental campaigns.

Pokhara Metropolitan City: tourism-based city, waste and environmental pressure, sustainability challenge at metropolitan scale.

Together, these municipalities provide a meaningful comparative frame for examining strategic leadership across different institutional and political contexts.

3.4 Quantitative Component: Comparative Leadership-Governance Scorecard

To introduce a quantitative element, this study developed an author-constructed Municipal Strategic Leadership and Governance Scorecard. Each municipality was assessed on five dimensions:

1. Strategic vision and agenda setting
2. Transparency and accountability
3. Citizen participation and co-creation
4. Service innovation and managerial responsiveness
5. Sustainability orientation

Each dimension was scored from 1 to 5, where 1 indicates very weak evidence and 5 indicates very strong evidence. The scoring is not an official ranking; rather, it is an interpretive tool derived from documented initiatives, public reporting, policy evidence, and literature-based assessment. Its purpose is to aid structured comparison.

3.5 Qualitative Analysis

The qualitative material was coded around five themes: visionary leadership, governance change, accountability mechanisms, participatory governance, and sustainability outcomes. Municipal examples were then interpreted in relation to the broader literature on public value, strategic leadership, and local government reform.

3.6 Limitations

This study relies heavily on secondary sources and therefore does not replace direct fieldwork, interviews, or citizen surveys. The scorecard is interpretive and should be read as analytical rather than definitive. Media reports were used cautiously, mainly to document current examples, while scholarly and institutional sources were used to frame the analysis. Despite these limits, the mixed-method design provides a credible and policy-relevant overview.

4.3 Kathmandu Metropolitan City: Disruptive Strategic Leadership

Kathmandu Metropolitan City provides the clearest example of strategic leadership as agenda disruption. Under Balendra Shah, the municipality became a national symbol of anti-establishment urban politics. The leadership contribution here has been strongest in three areas: agenda visibility, administrative assertiveness, and symbolic accountability. Live-streaming of meetings and the establishment of a citizen grievance mechanism signaled a move toward greater transparency. The mayor’s use of direct communication and visible enforcement also changed citizen expectations about what a metropolitan government can do.

From a corporate governance perspective, Kathmandu’s leadership contributed to sharper oversight, improved visibility of decisions, and stronger pressure on urban compliance. However, the sustainability of this governance style is mixed. Waste management remained highly politicized and technically difficult. Reports indicate that despite visible intervention, structural waste solutions such as composting, intergovernmental coordination, and stable contractor alignment did not fully materialize. This suggests that leadership was stronger in agenda-setting than in building durable systems.

Thus, Kathmandu illustrates both the power and the risk of strategic leadership. It can rapidly alter norms, energize citizens, and force bureaucratic movement, but if reforms are overly dependent on mayoral personality rather than institutional routines, the corporate sustainability of governance remains uncertain.

4.4 Dharan: Participatory Leadership and the Limits of Charisma

Dharan under Harka Sampang represents a different model. Strategic leadership here has been grounded less in formal systems and more in public mobilization. The water crisis became the defining governance issue, and the mayor responded through community-centered shramdaan. This approach generated legitimacy because it connected directly with a visible public problem and invited citizens into action rather than treating them as passive recipients.

Qualitatively, Dharan is perhaps the strongest case of participatory leadership among the selected municipalities. It demonstrates the co-creation logic discussed by Sørensen, Bryson, and Crosby (2021): public value can be generated when leadership mobilizes collective action. The very visible citizen participation in water-related works changed the public meaning of local leadership from bureaucratic office to civic movement.

Yet the corporate governance contribution is more ambivalent. Dharan’s model appears strong on participation but weaker on formal systems, procedural accountability, and managerial routinization. The refusal of external financing for water projects, while politically resonant, also raises questions about long-term fiscal and technical sustainability. A municipality cannot rely indefinitely on charisma, volunteerism, and moral energy. Therefore, Dharan shows that strategic leadership can transform public trust and mobilization, but without system-building it may struggle to deliver durable corporate sustainability.

4.5 Lalitpur: Incremental Leadership and Institutional Sustainability

Lalitpur under Chiri Babu Maharjan provides a strong example of strategic leadership through continuity rather than disruption. Unlike Kathmandu and Dharan, Lalitpur’s leadership style has been relatively less confrontational and more institutionally embedded. The municipality has pursued smart lighting, cycling lanes, pond restoration, heritage conservation, and earthquake-resilient reconstruction. Public reporting suggests that 61 historic rest houses have been reconstructed and that the city completed thousands of development projects in earlier years. Lalitpur has also pursued “green and blue” urban restoration by reviving ponds and water flows.

From a corporate governance standpoint, Lalitpur performs well because leadership appears to be aligned with institutional routines. Rather than depending on singular symbolic acts, the municipality has pursued a portfolio of interventions that connect urban heritage, environment, mobility, and city identity. This reflects an important aspect of strategic leadership often overlooked in Nepal: consistency. Sustainable governance is not only about dramatic reform; it is also about sequencing, continuity, and system stewardship.

Lalitpur’s contribution to corporate sustainability is comparatively strong. Its leadership appears to have balanced environmental restoration, public infrastructure, heritage protection, and longer-term urban branding. The main limitation is that incremental leadership can be less visible and may underperform in citizen perception when compared to highly charismatic mayors. Even so, in institutional terms, Lalitpur may be one of the strongest cases of governance change that is more likely to outlive a single political moment.

4.6 Dhangadhi: Managerial Strategic Leadership

Dhangadhi under Gopal Hamal demonstrates a more managerial model of strategic leadership. Evidence from UNDP Nepal and related case reporting suggests that the mayor has institutionalized daily management meetings, focused on accountability across departments, and prioritized public health, sanitation, anti-plastic campaigns, drainage, and civic responsibility. This is a less theatrical and more managerial form of strategic leadership than in Kathmandu or Dharan.

The corporate governance contribution of Dhangadhi is notable. Daily review meetings strengthen internal coordination, which is an essential governance principle but often absent in Nepali municipalities. Public health campaigns for low-income women and senior citizens also show a leadership attempt to connect governance with social sustainability. Plastic restrictions and drainage-focused interventions indicate that environmental and service governance have been treated as operational priorities rather than symbolic slogans.

However, Dhangadhi’s challenges remain those of scale and system depth. A municipality may hold daily meetings, but if data systems, internal monitoring, and fiscal planning remain weak, gains may be difficult to scale. Even so, among the selected municipalities, Dhangadhi presents one of the strongest examples of strategic leadership contributing to corporate governance through managerial discipline and operational accountability.

4.7 Pokhara: Sustainability Ambition under Implementation Pressure

Pokhara is a major tourism city and one of Nepal’s most environmentally sensitive metropolitan spaces. Under current leadership, the city has expressed commitment toward zero waste, waste segregation, recycling partnerships, and landfill development. Reports indicate that Pokhara generates around 188 tons of municipal waste daily, while plastic waste remains a major challenge and recycling rates remain modest. The metropolitan city’s efforts to coordinate with private recyclers and development partners show strategic recognition of the issue.

From a strategic leadership perspective, Pokhara’s strength lies in problem recognition and multi-stakeholder orientation. The leadership understands that waste, tourism, lakes, and city branding are interdependent. This is an important feature of strategic leadership: the capacity to frame issues systemically rather than sectorally. Yet the governance contribution remains incomplete because implementation is complex. Environmental sustainability in Pokhara depends on household segregation, transport logistics, landfill governance, regulation of private actors, and protection of tourism-sensitive natural assets.

Therefore, Pokhara’s leadership contribution to corporate sustainability is promising but still partial. It demonstrates strategic orientation, but the municipality has not yet fully converted that orientation into an integrated, highly visible governance system. In policy terms, Pokhara exemplifies the gap between sustainability ambition and municipal execution capacity.

4.8 Cross-Case Interpretation

The five cases reveal three broad patterns.

First, strategic leadership clearly matters in changing governance. In all five municipalities, leadership influenced agenda-setting, public expectations, and policy attention. This supports the broader literature that leadership is crucial in local government reform.

Second, not all leadership contributes to corporate governance in the same way. Kathmandu and Dharan show the strengths of disruptive and participatory leadership, but also the risk of over-personalization. Lalitpur and Dhangadhi show stronger integration between leadership and systems. Pokhara shows how sustainability-oriented leadership may still struggle if institutional machinery is weak.

Third, corporate sustainability is more difficult to achieve than political popularity. Environmental sustainability, financial sustainability, and institutional continuity all require administrative systems, not only a popular mayor. This is the most important critical insight of the study.

5.0 Discussion, Conclusion and Implications

5.1 Discussion

The evidence reviewed in this paper suggests that strategic leadership has become one of the most visible drivers of municipal governance change in Nepal. The selected municipalities show that strong local leadership can improve responsiveness, energize citizens, create new urban narratives, and challenge administrative inertia. This is especially clear in the post-2022 context, where mayors in several cities used public legitimacy to reposition local government as a serious site of reform. In this sense, strategic leadership has definitely contributed to changing governance in Nepal’s municipalities.

However, the study also shows that the contribution of current leadership to corporate governance and sustainability is uneven and incomplete. The strongest effect of strategic leadership so far has been on agenda setting and symbolic reform. Citizens increasingly expect mayors to be visible, decisive, responsive, and reform-oriented. In Kathmandu, this took the form of direct urban intervention and digital transparency. In Dharan, it took the form of citizen mobilization around water. In Dhangadhi, it appeared in disciplined administrative review. In Lalitpur, it emerged through continuity and steady urban stewardship. In Pokhara, it took the form of a sustainability agenda focused on waste and environmental management.

Yet, if corporate governance is understood as transparency, accountability, institutional coordination, risk control, documentation, and sustainable systems, then the results are more mixed. Han et al. (2024) are especially relevant here: transparency and responsiveness matter for trust, but trust alone does not automatically create institutional sustainability. Municipal leadership in Nepal often succeeds at restoring public confidence, but it does not always succeed at building the durable structures required for long-term governance.

The cases also reveal an important distinction between charismatic and institutional leadership. Independent mayors have been particularly effective at disrupting old political patterns, creating public energy, and forcing issues onto the policy agenda. However, they sometimes face greater friction with the bureaucracy, higher-level governments, and formal systems. Party-based leaders, on the other hand, may appear less dramatic but can sometimes deliver steadier continuity and intergovernmental negotiation. Lalitpur illustrates this more institutional pathway.

This means that the real test of strategic leadership in municipalities is not whether a mayor becomes popular, but whether the municipality becomes stronger as an institution. If a city’s governance improves only while one leader is in office, the reform is politically important but institutionally fragile. Corporate sustainability requires reforms to be embedded in procedures, staffing, data systems, financial planning, interdepartmental coordination, and citizen-interface mechanisms.

Another important point emerging from the analysis is that sustainability must be understood in multiple dimensions. Municipal discourse in Nepal often reduces sustainability to waste management or greenery. But the cases show that sustainability is also fiscal, managerial, and social. Thapa et al. (2024) show that many Nepali municipalities remain only moderately healthy financially. Therefore, even where leadership is socially vibrant, the municipality may still be vulnerable if it lacks long-term solvency and fiscal autonomy. Similarly, a highly participatory model such as Dharan’s may generate strong social legitimacy, but unless it is supported by technical and financial systems, it may not remain sustainable over time.

The findings also support the relevance of public value governance. Bryson et al. (2014) argue that public leadership today must work in a networked, multi-actor environment. The Nepali municipal cases validate this argument. No mayor can govern effectively through municipal office alone. Strategic leadership increasingly depends on the ability to work with citizens, technical staff, ward representatives, private firms, informal actors, development agencies, and higher tiers of government. The strongest municipal leaders are therefore not merely commanding figures; they are platform-builders.

5.2 Conclusion

This study concludes that strategic leadership has played a major role in changing the governance of municipalities in Nepal, including Kathmandu Metropolitan City, Dharan, Lalitpur, Dhangadhi, and Pokhara. Current local leadership has contributed significantly to agenda change, civic visibility, public trust, and issue prioritization. In that sense, municipalities in Nepal are no longer being judged only by roads and drains; they are increasingly being judged by leadership quality, responsiveness, and governance innovation.

However, the contribution of current leadership to corporate governance and corporate sustainability is only partial. The strongest improvements have occurred in responsiveness, visibility, participation, and strategic signaling. More uneven progress has been seen in institutionalization, financial resilience, internal controls, and durable system-building. Lalitpur and Dhangadhi appear comparatively stronger in turning leadership into systems. Kathmandu and Dharan show how powerful leadership can be in changing political culture, but also how vulnerable reform becomes when it depends heavily on one leader’s personality. Pokhara demonstrates that sustainability requires far deeper administrative capacity than political commitment alone.

Therefore, the answer to the assignment question is both positive and critical. Yes, strategic leadership has substantially changed municipal governance in Nepal. Yes, current leadership has contributed to better governance and sustainability in several respects. But no, these contributions are not yet sufficient to claim that Nepali municipalities have fully institutionalized corporate governance or achieved strong corporate sustainability. The transition is real, but incomplete.

5.3 Policy Implications and Recommendations

The results of this study suggest several policy implications for municipalities in Nepal.

First, municipalities should institutionalize strategic planning beyond mayoral manifestos. Each municipality should maintain a publicly available strategic dashboard with annual targets for governance, service delivery, fiscal health, and sustainability. This would reduce overdependence on personal leadership style.

Second, local governments should adopt a municipal governance charter based on public-sector corporate governance principles. This charter should include standards on disclosure, meeting transparency, grievance handling, conflict of interest, performance monitoring, audit follow-up, and citizen reporting.

Third, chief administrative officers and department heads should be tied more closely to performance frameworks. Strong mayors cannot deliver sustainable governance if administrative leadership remains weak or unaligned.

Fourth, municipalities should develop institutional systems for citizen co-creation. Dharan has shown the power of participation, but this should be translated into formal mechanisms such as participatory budgeting, thematic citizen forums, ward-level monitoring committees, and digital feedback dashboards.

Fifth, sustainability indicators must go beyond slogans. Municipalities should measure at least five sustainability dimensions annually: own-source revenue growth, waste segregation rate, citizen grievance resolution time, public meeting disclosure, and inclusion of climate or resilience measures in urban planning.

Sixth, federal and provincial governments should support municipalities with stronger fiscal and technical autonomy. Without this, even strong leadership may fail. The financial sustainability problem identified in the literature suggests that local leadership needs supportive structural reform, not only motivational rhetoric.

Seventh, leadership development for local governments should be institutionalized nationally. Nepal would benefit from a mayoral and municipal leadership platform that allows cities to share lessons on digital governance, sustainability management, public trust, procurement reform, and urban resilience.

Finally, local leadership in Nepal should move from “heroic leadership” to “institution-building leadership.” The future of municipal governance will depend not on which mayor dominates the headlines, but on which municipality builds the strongest systems.

5.4 Final Reflection

In my view, the recent experience of municipalities in Nepal shows that leadership really does matter, but not in a simplistic way. Strategic leadership is powerful because it can reframe public expectations, bring urgency into governance, and restore citizen trust. At the same time, leadership alone is not enough. Municipalities become sustainable only when leadership is converted into process, culture, and institution. Therefore, the most successful local leaders in Nepal will be those who can combine visibility with systems, public legitimacy with due process, and short-term action with long-term sustainability.
